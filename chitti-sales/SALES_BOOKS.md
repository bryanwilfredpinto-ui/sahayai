# Chitti Sales — The 10 Sales Books

The canonical canon. Every tactic Chitti Sales teaches must trace back to one of these books — or be flagged as "general sales wisdom, not from one of the 10 books I work from." See [skills/GUARDRAILS.md](skills/GUARDRAILS.md) item 1.

For each book: title, author, the **one big idea**, and **three practical tactics reframed for an Indian MSME context** (kirana / salon / tutor / dhaba / freelancer / pharmacy / gym / MSME).

---

## 1. *How to Win Friends and Influence People* — Dale Carnegie (1936)

**One big idea.** People do not buy your product — they buy how you make them feel. Be **genuinely interested** in the other person; remember their name; talk in terms of their interests, not yours.

**Three tactics for the Indian MSME.**

1. **Remember the customer's child's name and ask about exams.** On the second visit, instead of "what do you need", open with "beta ka exam kaisa gaya?". A kirana owner who does this for ten regulars will get ten regulars who will not switch shops for a ₹5 price difference.
2. **Use the customer's first name on every visit.** A salon owner who says "Anjali ji, the usual?" beats a salon that calls her "ma'am". The cost is zero. The retention lift is real.
3. **Talk in terms of the customer's interests.** A tutor selling Maths to a parent does not say "I cover the syllabus thoroughly." She says "your son is good at the easy questions but loses 12 marks on word problems. I'll fix that in 8 weeks." The frame shifts from "what I sell" to "what the parent already worries about".

## 2. *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* — Robert Cialdini (1984)

**One big idea.** Six universal principles drive human compliance: **reciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity**. Each can be used to serve — or to manipulate. Chitti teaches the service version only.

**Three tactics for the Indian MSME.**

1. **Reciprocity, served not weaponised.** A pharmacy that gives a genuinely useful free service (BP check, sugar test, generic-vs-brand explainer) creates real reciprocity. A pharmacy that gives a "free gift" the customer did not want is creating an obligation trap — Chitti does not teach that.
2. **Social proof on the wall.** A small dhaba that frames a printed thali-photo wall of regular customers with names ("Kumar ji, Tuesday lunch since 2019") signals to the next walk-in that this place is a regular's place. No paid review needed.
3. **Real scarcity, never invented.** A salon's Saturday-evening slot is genuinely scarce. Say so honestly: "Saturday 6 pm has one slot this week — would you like it?" Do **not** say "only one slot left!" when there are five. See [skills/BOUNDARIES.md](skills/BOUNDARIES.md).

## 3. *SPIN Selling* — Neil Rackham (1988)

**One big idea.** In larger sales (anything where the customer is making a considered decision), **ask, do not pitch**. Four question types, in order: **Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff**. The customer should be doing 70 percent of the talking.

**Three tactics for the Indian MSME.**

1. **For a tutor's first meeting with a parent**: Situation ("how does your son currently study at home?") → Problem ("which subject is the weakest?") → Implication ("if Maths stays at 60 percent, what happens to Class-10 board options?") → Need-payoff ("if we got him to 80 in eight weeks, would that change which school he can apply to?"). The parent has talked themselves into the value before any price has been mentioned.
2. **For a freelance designer's discovery call**: Situation ("what does your current logo do well?") → Problem ("what stops you from putting it on your packaging?") → Implication ("if a customer can't tell your brand from the next shop, what does that cost you per month?") → Need-payoff ("if a 30-second-recognition logo lifted your repeat-customer rate by even 10 percent, would the project pay back?").
3. **For a pharmacy's wholesale-buyer pitch**: Same shape. Ask about their current stock-out rate (Situation), their margin (Problem), the cost of a stock-out on busy days (Implication), the value of a guaranteed weekly supply (Need-payoff).

## 4. *The Challenger Sale* — Matthew Dixon and Brent Adamson (2011)

**One big idea.** Top sales performers do not "build relationships" — they **teach, tailor, and take control**. They tell the customer something the customer did not know about their own business. They are warm but they push.

**Three tactics for the Indian MSME.**

1. **Teach the parent something they did not know.** A tutor who says "your son loses 12 marks not because he doesn't know the maths, but because he runs out of time — let me show you the question he should *skip* in the exam" has just taught the parent something. The parent now sees the tutor as an expert, not a vendor.
2. **Tailor the tactic to the customer.** A kirana owner pitching a society contract does not say "we are very reliable". He says "your society had three stock-outs of Maggi last Diwali — I have data, here is how I'd prevent that." Specific. Tailored.
3. **Take control of the price conversation.** A salon owner who says "the price is ₹800" and then waits is in a weak position. A Challenger salon owner says "the price for the cut + the head massage is ₹800. I do not separate them because the massage is what makes the cut sit right. Shall I get you started?" — moving the customer from price to action.

## 5. *Predictable Revenue* — Aaron Ross (2011)

**One big idea.** **Cold calling 2.0**: do not have the salesperson call random leads — have a "researcher" qualify the lead first and hand a warm context to the closer. Separate the roles. (For a one-person MSME, the user plays both roles — but on different days, with a different mindset.)

**Three tactics for the Indian MSME.**

1. **Research before reaching out.** Before a freelance designer sends a cold DM, she spends 10 minutes on the prospect's Instagram. She references something specific in her first line: "I noticed your menu redesign last month — the price column is hard to read in low light. I have a 2-line fix." The cold DM is no longer cold.
2. **Separate "list-building day" from "calling day".** A tutor's Monday is spent building a list of 30 parents whose children are in 6th-standard tuition centres nearby. Tuesday is the day she actually calls. Mixing the two days kills both — one brain mode for research, another for talking.
3. **The "ideal customer profile" exercise.** A kirana owner asks himself: who is my 80-percent margin customer? Family flats with kids and one car? Or PG students who buy Maggi at midnight? They are not the same person. Stock and pitch differently for each.

## 6. *Never Split the Difference* — Chris Voss (2016)

**One big idea.** Negotiation is not logic — it is **tactical empathy**. **Mirror** (repeat the last 2-3 words as a question). **Label** ("It seems like you are worried about quality"). The "no" is a starting point, not an ending point. And the **calibrated question** ("how am I supposed to do that?") shifts the burden of the impossible request back to the asker.

**Three tactics for the Indian MSME.**

1. **Mirror a discount demand.** Customer: "Bhaiya, ₹50 kam karo?" Owner: "₹50 kam?" (as a question, with a slight pause). The customer hears himself, often softens, sometimes withdraws — without the owner having to refuse.
2. **Label the unspoken worry.** A parent enquiring about tuition fees pauses uncomfortably. The tutor says: "It seems like the monthly fee is a stretch right now." The parent, finally seen, opens up: "Yes, my husband's salary was cut last month." The conversation can now go somewhere honest — quarterly payment? Sibling discount? — instead of a polite "we'll let you know."
3. **The calibrated question for the impossible distributor demand.** Distributor: "Margin 5 percent badhao." Owner: "How am I supposed to do that without raising your wholesale price?" The distributor now has to solve the problem, not the owner.

## 7. *Pitch Anything* — Oren Klaff (2011)

**One big idea.** Every pitch is a **frame contest**. You either control the frame or the customer does. **Status framing** (you are the prize, not the supplicant), **time framing** (you have other places to be), and **intrigue framing** (a short story that ends right before the punchline) keep you in control.

**Three tactics for the Indian MSME.**

1. **Time-frame the meeting.** "Aunty, I have 12 minutes — let me show you what changed for Rohan's mother last month, then you tell me if it's worth a longer chat." The tutor is in control of the clock. The parent now treats the 12 minutes as scarce.
2. **Status-frame on price.** When a customer asks for a discount, a Klaff-style answer is "I have a price for the customers who come every month, and a price for one-time customers. Which one are you?" The customer has to choose into a category, not negotiate down.
3. **Intrigue-frame a referral ask.** "There's a customer who introduced me to four of her friends in six months — I'll tell you why she did that, but only if you stay for one more cup of chai." The MSME owner now controls the second half of the conversation.

## 8. *To Sell is Human* — Daniel Pink (2012)

**One big idea.** Everyone is in sales — the doctor selling a treatment, the teacher selling attention, the founder selling a vision. The **new ABCs**: **Attunement** (see from the other person's perspective), **Buoyancy** (stay afloat through rejection), **Clarity** (frame the problem, not just the solution).

**Three tactics for the Indian MSME.**

1. **Attunement on the second visit.** A gym owner who notices the new member is wearing yesterday's t-shirt does not say "your gear is great." He says "your t-shirt is dry — you came straight after office without changing, right? Let me show you the locker that fits a laptop." The attunement is in the noticing.
2. **Buoyancy after a "no".** A freelance designer sends 30 cold DMs. 27 ignore her. 2 say no. 1 says yes. Pink's lesson: the 28 nos are *data*, not a verdict. The designer's job after the third no is not to question her worth — it is to ask "what did I learn from the first three?".
3. **Clarity over solution.** A pharmacist does not say "use generics, they are cheaper." He says "in your father's hypertension treatment, the molecule is the same in the ₹200 brand and the ₹70 generic — the difference is the box, not the medicine. Would you like to try the generic for a month and see?" The problem has been framed before any solution was offered.

## 9. *Crossing the Chasm* — Geoffrey Moore (1991)

**One big idea.** The first few customers (innovators, early adopters) and the next many (early majority) are **different people**. What worked for the first 5 will not work for the next 50. There is a **chasm** in between that most products fall into.

**Three tactics for the Indian MSME.**

1. **Your first five regulars are not your next fifty.** A new gym's first 5 members were friends of the owner, motivated by the personal trainer relationship. The next 50 will be locals who care about machines, hygiene, and AC. The pitch that won the friends will not win the locals. Re-design the welcome experience for the second cohort.
2. **Pick a beachhead niche.** A freelance designer who tries to sell to "all small businesses" sells to none. The Moore answer: pick one — say, dental clinics in Pune — and become *the* designer for dental clinics. The next 50 dental clinics will come through referrals from the first 5.
3. **The "whole product".** A tutor's first customers buy the tutor. The early majority will not — they buy the **whole product**: tutor + study material + a parent WhatsApp update + a mock-test schedule + a result-tracking sheet. The Moore lesson is to start packaging the whole product before the chasm hits.

## 10. *The Psychology of Selling* — Brian Tracy (1985)

**One big idea.** Selling is a **set of disciplines**, not a personality trait. Big habits: **the 100-call method**, the **presumptive close**, **goal-setting in writing**, and the **self-concept** of "I am a professional salesperson" before "I am a kirana owner / tutor / freelancer".

**Three tactics for the Indian MSME.**

1. **The 100-call method, MSME version.** A freelance designer who has not landed a client in three months commits to 100 outreach messages in 30 days. Not 100 quality messages — just 100 messages, period. The volume defeats the perfectionism. The discipline is the point.
2. **The presumptive close.** Instead of "do you want to enrol?", the tutor says: "Shall we start Monday at 5 pm or Tuesday at 6 pm?" The customer now picks **between two yeses**. Klaff's status-frame meets Tracy's presumptive close.
3. **Self-concept first.** A kirana owner who introduces himself as "main bas ek chhota dukandaar hoon" loses every negotiation before it starts. Tracy's lesson: rehearse "I am a professional retailer with 12 years of customer relationships in this neighbourhood." The self-concept changes how the customer is allowed to bargain.
